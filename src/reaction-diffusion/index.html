The two rules

Imagine two chemicals, A and B, on a grid. Both diffuse — they spread into neighbouring cells, blurring out. And where they meet they react: one molecule of A plus two of B becomes three of B. B eats A and makes more of itself — autocatalysis. Two more knobs finish the world: a feed rate f tops A back up everywhere, and a kill rate k removes B. That's all there is. No rule says "make a spot." The spots are a consequence.

∂A/∂t = DA∇²A − AB² + f(1−A)　　∂B/∂t = DB∇²B + AB² − (k+f)B

The reaction wants to make blobs of B; diffusion wants to smear them out; the feed and kill set the balance. Where those forces settle is the pattern.

Why feed and kill change everything

The little map in the controls is the phase diagram: every point is a pair of feed/kill values, and which point you're standing on decides whether you get spots, stripes, a maze, or dividing blobs. The boundaries between regimes are sharp — nudge f by a few thousandths and the whole character flips. That's the surprise reaction-diffusion keeps teaching: the same machine, a hair's-breadth apart in parameter space, produces wildly different worlds. Drag the crosshair around and watch it happen.

From a leopard's coat to a chemical clock

This isn't just pretty math. In 1952 Alan Turing proposed — in his last paper — that exactly this kind of two-chemical system is how a featureless embryo decides where to put stripes and spots. The same equations predict animal coats, seashell markings, the spacing of hair follicles. The pattern isn't stored anywhere; it falls out of the chemistry.

And it has a second face. Tune the same engine into an excitable regime and the patterns stop sitting still and start to travel — rotating spirals and expanding target rings. That's the Belousov–Zhabotinsky reaction, the mesmerizing chemical clock you may have seen spiralling across a petri dish. Spots and spirals look like different phenomena; they're the same two-chemicals-diffusing idea in different neighbourhoods of its own phase diagram. (This tool explores the stationary-pattern side; the travelling-wave side is a natural next addition.)
